# COM7401 Monday campus session guides — staff only

These ten guides deliver the shared numbered notes in Monday AM/PM order. They supersede contradictory sequencing and assessment statements in the legacy `Week *_*/Session Guide.md` files. The established routine gives research-active staff a repeatable teaching method: **prepare → model → guided practice → individual application → feedback**. Use plain-language prompts, a written response before public discussion, and one main activity per block. Each student leaves with the output in the [shared master](../../Shared%20Module%20Master/README.md).

**Timing basis:** the ranges below describe a **180-minute teaching core**, matching current session guides; CELCAT does not show end times. Use the [hour ledger](../../Assessment/Scheduled%20Hours%20Ledger.md) before publishing them as final timetabled guides. If a confirmed event is longer, use the supervised extension shown in the ledger; if shorter, amend the timetable rather than silently removing an outcome. Take breaks at 50–60 and 140–150 minutes. Offer printed and digital task versions and a quiet-writing route before group work.

## Week 1 AM — Research quality and impact

**Prepare:** Student Note 01; book Chapters 1–3, pp. 1–36; existing impact worksheet. Prepare one clearly labelled practice case and a model problem/evidence statement.

- **0–20:** Welcome; show the five-week output sequence and the agreed portfolio format. Ask students to write one computing problem they care about. Say: “What would we need to find out before claiming a solution works?”
- **20–50:** Explain research problem, evidence and quality using the browser-warning worked example. Model a weak product objective and a stronger comparative knowledge question; ask students to identify the difference.
- **60–80:** In pairs, annotate the practice case: problem, intervention, evidence, limitation and potential impact. Give a partially completed row to anyone who has not prepared.
- **80–140:** Groups of 3–5 complete the impact worksheet. Assign reader, sceptic and reporter roles; circulate and ask “What claim could this evidence actually support?”
- **150–180:** Sample two groups, correct overclaims, and ask each student for the 120-word provisional problem/evidence statement. Collect it as the exit output.

**Expected check:** significance is specific, and the evidence would test a claim. **Common trap:** an artefact or potential social benefit is presented as an already demonstrated research finding.

## Week 1 PM — Philosophy and ontology

**Prepare:** Student Note 02; book Chapter 6, pp. 82–96; ontology debate. Put “philosophical ontology ≠ domain ontology” on the board.

- **0–20:** Retrieval: students write what would count as evidence for their provisional question. Ask: “Which assumptions about the phenomenon sit behind that answer?”
- **20–50:** Model two legitimate studies of security-dashboard use, one focused on measurable task outcomes and one on analysts' interpretations. Explain ontology, epistemology and values without attaching a paradigm to a whole discipline.
- **60–80:** Students write a position and one credible alternative individually; peers test whether each position changes the needed evidence.
- **80–140:** Run the adapted ontology debate in groups of about five. Give half a realist claim and half a constructivist claim for the same scenario; appoint an evidence checker and a counterargument reporter. Debate in paired groups, then exchange roles so students can articulate both cases.
- **150–180:** Debrief what each position reveals and misses. Each student submits four sentences: question, position, evidence, serious counter-position.

**Expected check:** a justified choice for the student's study, not a universal claim. **Common trap:** confusing the philosophy term with computing ontology or assuming realism always means quantitative research.

## Week 2 AM — Funnel and research question

**Prepare:** Student Note 03 and shared master Slide Deck 03; book Chapter 4, pp. 39–57, especially §4.4 pp. 47–48; existing funnel worksheet. The legacy 28-slide deck and 14-slide pre-reading deck are staff references only.

- **0–20:** Ask each student to name one specific uncertainty in their Week 1 topic. Check whether it is a gap supported by a source or only a hunch.
- **20–50:** Model the funnel from broad security warning design to a bounded question. Demonstrate the “build versus finding” distinction: a prototype is part of a method, not the answer itself.
- **60–80:** Complete the first three funnel boxes together with a fictional example. Ask “Which word still needs defining?”
- **80–140:** Students complete their own six-box funnel, then peer-test question, evidence, feasibility and scope using the note's checks. Tutor circulates with the staff misconception key.
- **150–180:** Invite two revisions, explain why each is better, and collect the revised main question plus two-sentence evidence plan.

**Expected check:** the question admits an evidence-based answer. **Common trap:** “no one has studied this” without a search trail, or a question that is merely a build specification.

## Week 2 PM — Literature appraisal

**Prepare:** Student Note 04; book Chapter 5, pp. 59–79; three accessible practice abstracts; the existing comparison chart as reference. PRISMA is optional.

- **0–20:** Retrieve the research question and ask for one source that might answer part of it. Display a descriptive summary and a critical comparison; students identify the difference.
- **20–50:** Model one evidence-matrix row using a practice abstract. Explain how the paper's method limits its claim and why “published” does not mean “directly relevant.”
- **60–80:** Pairs complete a second row, with a tutor prompt: “What is the strongest conclusion this paper warrants?”
- **80–140:** Students use three real topic sources where possible; offer practice abstracts while a source is found. They complete a matrix and write a 180-word comparative paragraph. Peers mark comparison, gap and source quality.
- **150–180:** Read one anonymised paragraph, improve its synthesis in front of the class, and collect each student's matrix and paragraph.

**Expected check:** sources are compared and tied to a justified gap. **Common trap:** three isolated summaries or treating a design proposal as effectiveness evidence.

## Week 3 AM — Design and ethics

**Prepare:** Student Note 05; book Chapter 6, pp. 97–112; completed ethics self-assessment; current local ethics guidance. The book's clearance examples are not local rules.

- **0–20:** Ask students to draw question → evidence → method from their Week 2 work. Identify where ethics could change the design.
- **20–50:** Model a design-and-creation evaluation and a mixed-method warning study. Show exactly where quantitative and qualitative evidence would be integrated.
- **60–80:** Guided case triage: students choose between experiment, case study, document analysis and design-and-creation, and explain what each can establish.
- **80–140:** Students complete a one-page design chain and discuss three specific risks from their ethics worksheet in pairs. Circulate to check consent, harm, access, storage and feasibility; do not promise ethical approval.
- **150–180:** Compare one strong and one weak method rationale, then collect the individual design chain and mitigation statement.

**Expected check:** method and safeguard fit actual proposed data. **Common trap:** listing “quantitative plus qualitative” without integration, or adding generic ethics boilerplate after the method.

## Week 3 PM — Sampling and collection

**Prepare:** Student Note 06; book Chapter 7, pp. 115–129; existing data collection worksheet as a reference; example of a weak and a corrected instrument item.

- **0–20:** Retrieve each student's question and unit of analysis. Ask: “What, exactly, would one row in your dataset or one interview account represent?”
- **20–50:** Model a sampling decision and instrument item for a security-warning study. Distinguish primary from secondary data and access from permission.
- **60–80:** Guided check of a double-barrelled survey question; students rewrite it and name the construct it measures.
- **80–140:** Students fill the five-row data-source table, draft one instrument item or measure, and peer-check whether it could answer the question.
- **150–180:** Tutor samples one plan from each method family, corrects population overclaims, and collects the individual table plus item.

**Expected check:** source, selection and instrument form a feasible chain. **Common trap:** convenience sampling presented as representative or actual recruitment during a proposal task.

## Week 4 AM — Quantitative analysis choice

**Prepare:** Student Note 07; book Chapter 7, pp. 130–137; invented CSV; existing quantitative cheat sheet as reference. Verify the worked calculations in the staff key.

- **0–20:** Ask students what a row and variable represent in the sample CSV. Poll or show hands on whether a count, median or regression answers each sample question.
- **20–50:** Model correct-flag counts and median time. Explicitly separate description of these ten cases from a claim about all users or causation.
- **60–80:** Pairs calculate the second interface and write one warranted and one unwarranted conclusion.
- **80–140:** Students choose an analysis for their own proposed quantitative data, or critique the sample plan if their proposal is wholly qualitative. Give method-choice feedback rather than a software tutorial.
- **150–180:** Compare choices, revisit assumptions and collect the named analysis, data type and bounded interpretation.

**Expected check:** analysis answers the question and matches the data. **Common trap:** selecting a fashionable technique without enough data or a coherent design.

## Week 4 PM — Qualitative analysis choice

**Prepare:** Student Note 08 and shared master Slide Deck 08; book Chapter 6, pp. 109–111 and Chapter 7, pp. 133–135; invented transcript. The legacy thematic slides and Qual Cheat Sheet are references, not the exercise.

- **0–20:** Ask what the practice question seeks to understand and why a frequency count alone may miss it.
- **20–50:** Model two initial codes on the fictional transcript and trace a quotation → code → candidate theme. State that another interpretation must be considered.
- **60–80:** Pairs code two lines independently and explain differences in labels.
- **80–140:** Groups code the remaining lines and propose a theme with supporting and disconfirming evidence. Individuals then write how their own planned qualitative material would be analysed and protected.
- **150–180:** Compare two plausible coding approaches; collect each student's coding table, theme, rival reading and method choice.

**Expected check:** theme is a defensible interpretation. **Common trap:** calling a repeated word a theme or implying invented teaching comments are research findings.

## Week 5 AM — Report integration and reflection

**Prepare:** Student Note 09; book Chapters 3 and 5; current Word brief/rubric; peer-review checklist. Schedule this *before* that track's report lock.

- **0–20:** Display the four required headings and the same question running through them. Ask students to circle any inconsistent wording of their question.
- **20–50:** Model a literature-gap → method → reflection chain. Explain one genuine design trade-off and a matching rigour check.
- **60–80:** Pairs exchange drafts with two written strengths, two actionable revisions and one question about evidence–method fit.
- **80–140:** Protected revision time; tutor triages questions, particularly ethics, analysis match and referencing. Students complete their declaration and submission checklist.
- **150–180:** Each student records a revision list and reflection on a specific limitation; check the relative report deadline and lock route.

**Expected check:** coherent, feasible proposal. **Common trap:** reflection is a recap or the report claims empirical findings not obtained.

## Week 5 PM — Methodological defence and transfer

**Prepare:** Student Note 10; approved Word brief and four-check reference guide. This session follows report lock and check release. If either has not happened, practise only with unassigned sample checks.

- **0–20:** Confirm what is locked, the assigned check, four-minute ±30-second limit, declaration and no-GAI rule for the defence.
- **20–50:** Model a response to a *practice* question. Show how it applies a check rather than summarises the report.
- **60–80:** In pairs, students give a 60-second question–method explanation; partner asks for the missing evidence link.
- **80–140:** Timed practice rounds and recording/technical support. Use quiet spaces or independent recording if available; do not require public performance.
- **150–180:** Resolve access/upload issues, collect a self-check on question, named check, direct response and time. Confirm the relative defence deadline.

**Expected check:** student can defend the exact submitted question and method. **Common trap:** rehearsing a generic pitch or using the book's panel-viva format instead of this assessment.
