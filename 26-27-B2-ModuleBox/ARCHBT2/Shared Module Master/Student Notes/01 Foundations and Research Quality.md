# 01 — Foundations and research quality

**Goal:** explain why a computing problem is worth researching and what evidence would make a proposed answer credible. This topic supports LO1 and the portfolio's Background section.

Research begins with a question whose answer is not yet established. A useful proposal connects a real problem, what is already known, a defensible gap and a feasible way of investigating it. Building software can be part of research, but the build alone is not the finding: the proposal must explain what will be learned by evaluating or using the artefact. Research in computer science, information systems and cybersecurity often overlaps; choose a method for the *question*, not for the discipline label.

**Worked example.** A team can build a browser warning that flags suspicious login pages. A research question would ask whether the warning helps a specified group recognise phishing more accurately than an existing warning, and what evidence would show that. The comparison, measures and participant safeguards matter as much as the prototype.

**Quality check.** Ask whether the problem is specific, the proposed evidence fits it, the study is ethical and feasible, and another researcher could understand how the conclusion would be reached. A narrow, well-justified question is usually more useful here than a broad claim to solve “cybersecurity.”

**Glossary:** *research problem* — the gap or difficulty to investigate; *evidence* — material used to support a conclusion; *feasibility* — whether the planned work can be done with the available time, access and resources; *artefact* — a designed product such as an application or model.

**Your task:** write up to 120 words naming your provisional problem, why it matters, what is already uncertain and the kind of evidence that could answer it. Keep this statement for the Week 2 funnel exercise. You are planning a study; you do not need to collect data now.
